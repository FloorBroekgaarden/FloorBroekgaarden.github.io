<p style="margin: 0; font-size: 0.97em; line-height: 1.75; font-style: italic;"><strong>Mission:</strong> Our mission is to open and lead the new frontier of gravitational-wave paleontology: reconstructing the history of massive stars and compact objects across cosmic time using gravitational waves as cosmic fossils. We seek to transform gravitational-wave astronomy from the discovery of individual events into a precision population science capable of revealing how massive stars form, evolve, interact, explode, and shape the Universe. By combining astrophysics, AI, statistics, and large-scale computation, our group develops the tools, simulation ecosystems, and uncertainty-aware frameworks needed to connect gravitational-wave observations back to their progenitor stars and tackle the fundamental "simulation silo" challenge in stellar evolution. Through interdisciplinary, student-driven research and a strong commitment to open and collaborative science, we aim to build a new framework for understanding the dynamic Universe; from the first generations of stars to the black holes and neutron stars merging today.</p>
</div>

<div style="font-size: 0.97em; line-height: 1.75; margin-bottom: 2em; max-width: 860px;">

<p>The Gravitational-Wave Paleontology Group studies the formation, lives, and explosive deaths of massive stars across cosmic time using gravitational waves as "cosmic fossils." When black holes and neutron stars merge, they produce ripples in spacetime that carry information about the stars that formed them billions of years earlier. By studying these mergers, we aim to answer some of the biggest open questions in astrophysics today: How do black holes and neutron stars form? Which evolutionary pathways produce the gravitational-wave sources we observe? And what can these mergers teach us about the lives, environments, and deaths of massive stars throughout the history of the Universe? This emerging field — gravitational-wave paleontology — opens an entirely new way of studying stellar evolution, allowing us to probe populations of massive stars that are otherwise inaccessible to traditional light-based astronomy because they lived in the distant, early Universe or died long ago.</p>

<p>We are entering the Big Data era of gravitational-wave astronomy. Over the next decade, detectors such as LIGO, Virgo, Cosmic Explorer, the Einstein Telescope, and LISA will increase the number of observed compact-object mergers from hundreds today to millions per year, extending observations to the edge of the observable Universe. However, interpreting this wealth of data requires overcoming a major challenge: gravitational waves do not directly reveal the properties of the progenitor stars that formed them. Instead, connecting observations back to stellar evolution requires large-scale theoretical simulations that model how massive binaries evolve over billions of years. At present, these simulations are computationally expensive, highly uncertain, and often fragmented into isolated "simulation silos," where different groups explore only narrow regions of parameter space within individual codes. These silos obscure uncertainties, bias physical interpretation, and limit our ability to learn robustly from gravitational-wave observations.</p>

<p>Our group tackles this challenge by combining astrophysics, AI/ML, statistics, and scientific computing to build new uncertainty-aware frameworks, simulation catalogs, and data-driven tools that can transform gravitational-wave detections into a quantitative fossil record of massive-star evolution across cosmic time. Central to this effort is the development of <strong>GROWL</strong> (<em>GRavitatiOnal Wave paLeontology</em>), a next-generation, community-driven simulation ecosystem that will unify thousands of simulations across formation channels, stellar evolution codes, and physical assumptions into a single interoperable framework. By breaking today's fragmented "simulation silos," GROWL aims to make it possible, for the first time, to robustly connect observed gravitational-wave populations back to their progenitor stars and directly test which physical processes — such as mass transfer, supernovae, stellar winds, metallicity, and dynamical interactions — shape the black holes and neutron stars we observe across the Universe. Through this framework, our group seeks to answer some of the defining questions of modern astrophysics: How do gravitational-wave sources form? How did massive stars live and die in the early Universe? And what can black hole and neutron star mergers reveal about the cosmic history of stars, galaxies, and chemical enrichment?</p>

<p>Our research spans scales from the interiors of massive stars to the evolution of galaxies across cosmic time. We develop large-scale gravitational-wave simulation catalogs, AI-driven statistical emulators, interactive visualization tools, and next-generation inference frameworks capable of exploring the enormous uncertainty landscape of stellar evolution. The group investigates some of the most uncertain and influential processes in astrophysics — including mass transfer, common envelope evolution, stellar winds, supernova explosions, compact-object formation, and chemical enrichment — while connecting gravitational-wave populations to electromagnetic observations across the multi-messenger Universe. Our projects range from black hole population synthesis, cosmological enrichment histories, and kilonova modeling to the science case of future observatories such as Cosmic Explorer, the Einstein Telescope, and LISA.</p>

<p>The lab is deeply interdisciplinary and student-driven, bringing together researchers from astrophysics, data science, statistics, AI/ML, and scientific computing to develop both new scientific insight and new computational methodologies. Students in the group work at the frontier of gravitational-wave astronomy while gaining experience in high-performance computing, machine learning, visualization, statistical inference, and open-source scientific software development. By combining theory, computation, machine learning, and multi-messenger astrophysics, the lab aims not only to advance gravitational-wave astronomy, but to establish gravitational-wave paleontology as a foundational new frontier for understanding how massive stars shaped the Universe across cosmic history.</p>

</div>

<h2>Research themes</h2>

<p style="font-size: 0.95em; color: #555;">Click a theme to read more and see example projects.</p>

<div class="research-themes">

  <details class="research-theme" id="simulations">
    <summary>Simulating billions of stars and the black holes &amp; neutron stars they form</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <img src="/images/research_compas.png" alt="Illustration of simulated binary star evolution with COMPAS">
    <p>We use rapid population synthesis codes, primarily <a href="https://compas.science">COMPAS</a>, to simulate large populations of binary stars and follow how massive stars evolve into black holes and neutron stars. By running millions of simulations across many different physical assumptions, we map the large uncertainties in stellar evolution and predict merger populations that can be compared directly with gravitational-wave observations.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Large grids of binary population synthesis simulations with <a href="https://compas.science">COMPAS</a> across many physical assumptions</li>
      <li>Efficient sampling of rare outcomes, such as with <a href="/software/">STROOPWAFEL</a> (adaptive importance sampling)</li>
      <li>Building <strong>GROWL</strong>, a catalog that brings together simulations from many codes and groups to break "simulation silos"</li>
      <li>Merger rates and how they depend on uncertain physics, e.g. <a href="/publications/">"Lower Your Rates"</a> on claims of a binary black hole rate crisis</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="star-formation-history">
    <summary>Investigating the cosmic star formation history using gravitational waves</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>When and where black holes and neutron stars merge depends on how and where their progenitor stars formed. The redshift distribution of gravitational-wave mergers therefore encodes the history of star formation and chemical enrichment across cosmic time. We combine population synthesis with models of the metallicity-dependent star formation history, from simple analytical prescriptions to cosmological simulations, to learn how the conditions for forming merging compact objects changed over billions of years.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Comparing analytical star formation history models with cosmological simulations and their impact on merger populations (<a href="/publications/#levina2026sfh">Levina et al. 2026</a>)</li>
      <li>Cosmic integration: turning simulated binaries into predicted merger rates across redshift</li>
      <li>Using the growing gravitational-wave catalogs (e.g. GWTC-4 and GWTC-5) to learn about star formation in the early Universe</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="binary-stars">
    <summary>Investigating the lives of binary stars</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Most massive stars live in binaries, and interactions between the two stars, such as mass transfer, common envelope evolution, stellar winds and supernova kicks, largely decide whether a binary ends up as a merging pair of compact objects. These phases are some of the most uncertain in astrophysics. We use gravitational-wave observations, together with electromagnetic observations of binaries in our own Galaxy, as a new and independent way to constrain them.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>How often common envelope evolution forms gravitational-wave sources (<a href="/publications/">Broekgaarden et al. 2026</a>)</li>
      <li>Mass ratio reversals and their impact on binary black hole masses and rates (<a href="/publications/">Smith et al. 2026</a>)</li>
      <li>Stellar winds, supernova kicks and stripped stars, and their imprint on compact-object populations</li>
      <li>Interactive visualizations of evolving massive stars, such as <a href="/software/">VIMES</a></li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="white-dwarfs">
    <summary>White dwarfs and white dwarf mergers</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>White dwarfs are the most common stellar remnants and keep a record of how their progenitor stars lived and interacted. White dwarfs in close binaries let us study poorly understood phases such as common envelope evolution, for example through white dwarf + M dwarf binaries in star clusters, where the age and initial mass of the system can be measured. Merging double white dwarfs are also prime sources for the space-based detector LISA and possible progenitors of Type Ia supernovae. We model how these systems form and use them to test binary evolution in a different mass range from black holes and neutron stars.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Linking pre- and post-common envelope binaries in star clusters, starting with a massive white dwarf + M dwarf binary in Alessi 12 (<a href="/publications/#grondin2026ce">Grondin et al. 2026</a>)</li>
      <li>Double white dwarf populations as sources for LISA, including gravitational-wave sources in our Galactic backyard</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="transients">
    <summary>Transients: Type Ia supernovae, short gamma-ray bursts, and kilonovae</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Merging compact objects do not only produce gravitational waves: they can also power some of the brightest explosions in the Universe. Neutron star mergers can launch short gamma-ray bursts and kilonovae, the radioactive glow that forges heavy elements, and merging or accreting white dwarfs are leading candidates for Type Ia supernovae. We model where, when and how often these transients occur, and connect these predictions to observations of the transients and their host galaxies to learn about the binaries that produce them.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Predicting the rates, properties and host-galaxy offsets of merger-driven gamma-ray bursts across cosmic time using COMPAS and cosmological models (<strong>Joseph Rodriguez</strong>)</li>
      <li>Kilonova modeling and fast inference with emulators of radiative transfer simulations, to learn about the origin of heavy elements (<strong>Marko Ristić</strong>)</li>
      <li>Multi-messenger prospects for black hole–neutron star and neutron star mergers in current and future observing runs</li>
      <li>White dwarf binary populations as progenitors of Type Ia supernovae, compared with observed supernova rates and delay times</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="delay-times-enrichment">
    <summary>Delay time distributions and (r-process) chemical enrichment</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>The time between the formation of a binary and its merger or explosion, its delay time, sets when and where elements are released into their surroundings. Neutron star mergers are a key site of the rapid neutron-capture (r-) process that produces elements such as gold and europium, while Type Ia supernovae enrich galaxies with iron. We model the delay time distributions of these sources and connect them to the chemical abundances of stars, from the oldest and smallest galaxies to the Milky Way, to test which sources enriched the Universe and when.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Delay time distributions of compact-object mergers and Type Ia supernovae from population synthesis</li>
      <li>R-process enrichment of ultra-faint dwarf galaxies (UFDs), and whether neutron star mergers can enrich them quickly enough</li>
      <li>Chemical signatures of disrupted dwarf galaxies in the Milky Way's stellar halo</li>
      <li>Disentangling Type Ia supernova and binary neutron star contributions to galactic chemical evolution</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="ai-statistics">
    <summary>Developing statistical, AI, and ML techniques for stellar populations &amp; gravitational waves</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Connecting thousands of gravitational-wave detections to high-dimensional stellar evolution models requires new computational tools. We develop Bayesian hierarchical inference methods, emulators and machine-learning approaches that make it possible to explore the full parameter space of these models and to quantify the uncertainties in population analyses.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Bayesian hierarchical inference connecting gravitational-wave catalogs to stellar physics</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="ai-emulators">
    <summary>AI emulators for stellar populations and transients</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Detailed simulations of binary populations and of the light from explosive transients are expensive, which limits how many models we can run and compare with data. We train AI emulators (surrogate models) on these simulations so that new predictions take seconds instead of days. This lets us explore much larger parameter spaces and use the full simulations directly in inference on gravitational-wave and electromagnetic observations.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Generative emulators for gravitational-wave astrophysics and population synthesis (<strong>Rhea Senthil Kumar</strong>)</li>
      <li>Surrogate models of detailed radiative transfer simulations of kilonovae, for fast Bayesian inference of these transients and the origin of heavy elements (<strong>Marko Ristić</strong>)</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="parameter-spaces">
    <summary>Quantifying high-dimensional parameter spaces</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Predictions for gravitational-wave sources depend on dozens of uncertain physical choices, from mass transfer and winds to supernova kicks and star formation history, and different codes and groups make different choices. Each simulation explores only a small corner of this huge parameter space. We develop ways to map, organize and compare these high-dimensional spaces, so that we can see which assumptions matter most, where models agree or disagree, and which regions are still unexplored.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Using large language models (LLMs) to extract and standardize the metadata (physical assumptions, settings and outputs) of published simulations for the GROWL catalog</li>
      <li>Quantifying model uncertainties across codes and physical assumptions in GROWL</li>
      <li>Mapping which physical assumptions drive the biggest changes in predicted merger rates and properties</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="future-detectors">
    <summary>Future gravitational-wave detectors</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <img src="/images/research_gwdetectors.png" alt="Next-generation gravitational-wave detectors">
    <p>Next-generation observatories such as the <a href="https://www.et-gw.eu/">Einstein Telescope</a>, <a href="https://cosmicexplorer.org/">Cosmic Explorer</a> and LISA will detect hundreds of thousands of compact-object mergers per year, out to the edge of the observable Universe. We study what astrophysics these instruments will make possible and prepare the theoretical models and analysis frameworks needed to make full use of their data.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Science cases for the Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer, including multi-messenger prospects</li>
      <li>Predictions for black hole–neutron star mergers and kilonovae in current and upcoming observing runs</li>
      <li>Forecasts for how many and which sources future detectors will find</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="visualizations">
    <summary>Visualizations and animations</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Stellar evolution and gravitational-wave populations are high-dimensional and hard to grasp from static plots alone. We build interactive figures, animations and visualization tools that make our simulations easier to explore, both for researchers comparing models and for students and the public learning how massive stars live and die. See our <a href="/interactive-figures/">Animations and Interactive Figures</a> page.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li><a href="/interactive-figures/">VIMES</a> (Visualization of Massive Evolving Stars): animations following massive binaries through their evolution</li>
      <li>An interactive catalog of formation channel rates for compact-object mergers</li>
      <li>Interactive figures and tables accompanying papers, such as <em>Lower Your Rates</em></li>
      <li>Visualizations of gravitational-wave detections and detector horizons</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="open-science">
    <summary>Supporting software, big and open data, and reproducibility in science</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>Open and reproducible science is central to how we work. We release our simulations and analysis pipelines publicly so that results can be checked and built upon, contribute to open-source tools such as the <a href="https://www.tomwagg.com/software-citation-station/">Software Citation Station</a>, and work to improve how software is cited and credited in astronomy. See our <a href="/software/">Software and Data</a> page.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Public data releases of our simulations on <a href="/software/">Zenodo</a> alongside every paper</li>
      <li>Best practices and a practical primer on software citation (<a href="/publications/#vanlane2026software">Van-Lane et al. 2026</a>; <a href="/publications/#broekgaarden2026software">Broekgaarden et al. 2026</a>)</li>
      <li>Contributing to open-source codes such as COMPAS and the Software Citation Station</li>
    </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-theme" id="growl-task-force">
    <summary>The GROWL Task Force: doing science together with the community</summary>
    <div class="theme-body">
    <p>The GROWL Task Force is our initiative to open up gravitational-wave research to people beyond the lab. Students, industry professionals and anyone curious about gravitational waves can follow our work, get updates from the group, and contribute to real research problems alongside us. Participants from industry bring software and data-engineering expertise, students get mentorship and new career pathways, and everyone gets a firsthand look at how science is done.</p>
    <p class="theme-projects-label"><strong>Example projects:</strong></p>
    <ul class="theme-projects">
      <li>Community research projects on the GROWL catalog with students and industry participants</li>
      <li>Mentorship and onboarding of new members by students in the group</li>
      <li>Regular updates and behind-the-scenes stories from the lab</li>
    </ul>
    <p>Interested in joining? See the <a href="/join/#growl-task-force">Join the Lab</a> page, or reach out to Floor through our <a href="https://forms.gle/rr2kfbJ74pczVrvn7" target="_blank">interest form</a>.</p>
    </div>
